Seven months, waterfall. Launched on the planned date.

Each dot is one person on the team of 50. B2B customers reached at launch: 1M+.

The problem

Telefonica needed 5G live for its business customers across Germany. The service used a non-standalone architecture, running 5G through the existing LTE/4G core, so it had to be integrated with legacy systems. The work crossed many engineering teams and client stakeholders, on a fixed plan. Competitors had already launched, so sales and senior management wanted to move fast.

What I decided

  • Hold the scope. Sales teams and senior management pushed for rushed features and quick compromises to catch up with competitors. I said no to features that would look good in a demo but did not solve a real customer problem.
  • Raise the capability gap early. I saw that what the technology could deliver did not match what stakeholders expected. I raised it early, and we changed the scope before it could threaten the launch date.

What I owned

  • The launch, end to end. I led the team of 50 from planning to go-live.
  • The schedule. I aligned timing across stakeholders and kept the plan current.
  • The risks. I flagged risks early so they could be resolved before they reached the critical path.
  • The numbers. I defined the KPIs and ran the reporting to leadership.

How I worked

I ran the engineering team meetings and the ideation sessions with the customer, and kept collaboration between stakeholders moving throughout.

Result

5G NSA services launched across Germany on the planned date, reaching more than 1 million B2B customers. Across the wider Telefonica engagement, B2B mobile customers grew 15% and retention rose 10%.

The problem

Business customers on the All-IP fixed-line product had limited options. The goal was to add internet and voice features to the portfolio. The work was part of a company-wide release, so it had to land on a fixed date alongside several other projects.

What I did

  • Defined the scope. I gathered the high-level requirements, raised the service request, and ran the kickoff that set scope, timeline, milestones and initial risks.
  • Got agreement. I ran workshops with the network, CRM and other affected teams until every group agreed on the user stories and acceptance criteria, then secured sign-off.

The features went through technical launch, then business readiness and customer readiness testing in production. They launched commercially a month later and were handed over to operations. Internet and voice are now part of the All-IP portfolio for fixed-line business customers.

The problem

Wrodium is a content engineering platform that enterprises use to stay accurate and visible in AI search. The product was new, and it was not yet clear which capability clients valued most.

What I found

I analysed how clients used the product. Three things drew their interest: content analytics, which AI models cited them, and how they ranked against their competitors. I also reviewed what competing products offered.

What I decided

Build competitive ranking first. Clients wanted to know where they stood against their peers, and the product did not show it.

AI matching for the informal economy

The human-curated layer comes first because the model alone could not read local job language.

The problem

Young people in Mombasa describe their work in local terms: a “Fundi” is a mechanic or builder, a “Mama Mboga” sells vegetables. Tabiya's platform reads skills through the ESCO framework, and Kenyan employers use the national KeSCO classification. Without a bridge between the three, the platform could not match people to jobs.

What I decided

Curate before modelling. AI alone could not read the local language, so the first layer of the pipeline became a human-vetted answer key, with the model behind it.

What I did

  • Answer key. Managed a dataset of more than 100 vetted informal job titles and slang terms, mapped to their formal meaning.
  • NLP pipeline. Led a Python pipeline that turns KeSCO and ESCO occupation descriptions into embeddings and pairs them by cosine similarity.
  • Operating playbook. Wrote the deduplication rules for overlapping job codes and designed the impact evaluation for the pilot.

Result

Job-skill matching accuracy improved by 73%. A manual, error-prone mapping became an automated pipeline that can scale to tens of thousands of records.

Recommending healthier recipes

Data230,000+ crowdsourced Food.com recipes
MethodsNLP, Random Forest, Bayesian average ranking
ToolsPython
What 230,000 recipes showed
Healthier recipes
1x
Unhealthier recipes
3–5x
0x5x

Likelihood of using highly appetising keywords, with healthier recipes as the baseline. The shaded part of the bar is the range between three and five times.

The problem

Healthy recipes lose to comfort food on recipe platforms. The usual assumption is that comfort food means unhealthy food. We tested whether the gap was in the food or in the words used to describe it.

What I found

The gap is in the language. Healthy recipes are missing the appetising keywords that unhealthy recipes use three to five times more often. Healthy food has a branding problem that a platform can address.

What I decided

Ship the simpler ranking. We built and evaluated personalised recommenders using NLP and Random Forest models. On messy, user-generated data, a Bayesian average ranking was more effective at driving positive engagement, so we recommended it.

How we would measure it live

  • North star: recipe conversion rate, the share of users who save a recommended recipe or add it to a grocery list.
  • Engagement: click-through on healthy recipes in the top five slots, against the baseline.
  • Guardrails: session length and bounce rate, so healthier ranking does not cost browsing time.
  • Technical: ranking latency under 200 ms.

Client: UC Berkeley IT and Advising / UX research course / Spring 2026

Advising with AI in CalCentral

0 of 6

students we interviewed had noticed the advising feature in CalCentral. In our redesigned prototype, every test participant found and booked an advisor quickly.

RoleUX Researcher and Project Coordinator
TeamFour graduate and undergraduate students
UsersUC Berkeley undergraduates
Methods6 interviews, 27-student survey, 4 usability tests
Did students notice the advising feature?

0 of 6 interviewed students had noticed it. Each hollow circle is one student. In our redesigned prototype, every test participant found and booked an advisor quickly.

The problem

CalCentral is the portal Berkeley students open every day, but its advising feature sits in secondary menus. Students face high-stakes decisions about majors and internships with no clear path to an advisor.

What we found

  • Advising was invisible. None of the six students interviewed had noticed the advising card. 38.5% of survey respondents had never used advising.
  • Students want career help. 92.3% seek career or internship guidance from advisors.
  • Our AI hypothesis was wrong. We expected students to want AI to handle everything. 80.8% preferred AI together with a human advisor, and only 3.8% wanted AI first. Trust in AI for academic decisions averaged 5.4 out of 10.

What we decided

  • Fix visibility first. Move advising to the main dashboard.
  • Use AI for preparation only. The assistant helps students prepare for a meeting and summarises it afterwards. It does not make the decision.
  • Keep the advisor one click away. A direct booking button sits beside the assistant.
  • Change the research plan. Scheduling conflicts ruled out focus groups, so we ran a survey, which validated the interview findings at larger scale.

What we tested

We built a working React prototype and ran moderated think-aloud sessions with four students.

  • Every participant found and booked an advisor quickly. One did it “within 2 seconds”.
  • Labelling the AI as a preparation tool increased students' confidence in using it.
  • Students expected a calendar invite or email after booking, and two overlapping AI buttons caused confusion.
  • Several said the assistant would be most useful inside course enrolment, where they choose classes.

What we recommended

We presented three recommendations to Berkeley IT: raise advising in the dashboard hierarchy, launch assistive AI for meeting preparation and requirement checks, and keep complex decisions with human advisors. For a rollout we proposed A/B testing the placement, tracking advisor email volume over a year, and setting data privacy rules for student records first.
